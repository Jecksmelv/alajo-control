## 1. Nigeria financial inclusion

### CBN National Financial Inclusion Strategy (NFIS)
- The latest target is **70% formal financial inclusion**, which A2F 2026 reports as exceeded at 73%. The source is Nairametrics (above, high confidence). The earlier NFIS target of 80% by 2020 was not verified. A2F.ng's 2024 repost refers to a "95%" target for 2020, but I treat that as unreliable press wording.

## 2. Ajo / esusu / ROSCAs and daily collectors ("alajo")

| Fact | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| "**Seventeen percent of adults report savings in an informal savings group (adashi/esusu/ajo)**." About one in five adults and 38% of households report having a formal bank account. The data come from the GHS-Panel 2010/11, a nationally representative survey of 5,000 households and 14,800 adults. | NBS & World Bank (LSMS-ISA), "Use of Financial Services in Nigeria" brief (GHS-Panel 2010/2011). https://nigerianstat.gov.ng/download/147 | High (I read the sentence in the PDF text). The data are old. |
| CGAP: even when Nigerians have a bank account, "they often prefer the agility and convenience of informal mechanisms that are widespread in Nigeria," such as "savings collectors (e.g. alajo, akawo) or savings groups (e.g. Esusu)." | Antonique Koning (CGAP), "Banking on Including Women in Nigeria," reposted by Women's World Banking, 20 Dec 2013. https://www.womensworldbanking.org/insights/cgap-blogs-work-nigeria/ | High |
| GSMA 2024 report: "most" Nigerian women who save use informal groups like Ajo rather than mobile wallets. A 2023 GSMA survey found only **9%** of roughly 106 million Nigerian women use mobile money. Service downtime drives distrust. | BusinessDay (Chinwe Michael), "Nigerian women shun mobile money for traditional saving groups," 22 Feb 2024, reporting GSMA, *Women's Trust of Mobile Money and Agents in Nigeria and Senegal*. https://businessday.ng/technology/article/nigerian-women-shun-mobile-money-for-traditional-saving-groups/ | High (press report of GSMA) |
| An academic paper describes ajo/esusu as "the group-based savings system called Ajo or Esusu in the south-west of Nigeria," "an agelong platform for many women" built on "communities of trust." | Ada Ivy Phil-Ugochukwu, "Informal Financial Savings Practices to Facilitate Formal Financial Inclusion," *World Journal of Advanced Research and Reviews*, Dec 2024 (via FinDev Gateway). https://www.findevgateway.org/paper/2024/12/informal-financial-savings-practices-to-facilitate-formal-financial-inclusion | High (abstract page) |
| Further reading I did not read in full: an Erasmus University (ISS) research paper on esusu among Dugbe market traders in Ibadan (Akamere, 2024). | https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/75775/ | Not verified beyond the search snippet |

## 5. Optional: reconciliation and trust

- **Trust in informal vs digital savings:** The GSMA/BusinessDay item (section 2) reports that downtime-driven distrust leads women to withdraw funds when outage rumours spread. (High confidence)
- **Fraud risk with collectors:** A Nigerian property blog notes that a dishonest alajo "carries real fraud risk" with "no deposit insurance or regulatory protection." Source: https://ownkey.com/ng/blog/ajo-esusu-property-savings-nigeria. (Low confidence: a non-academic blog seen only as a search snippet. Use only as colour.)
- **Reconciliation cost:** I found no credible primary source. The figures I came across (AICPA, AFP, McKinsey, and Deloitte numbers quoted by vendor blogs) could not be traced to the original reports, so they are **not recorded**.
